My name is Nate.

In 2020, when I was eleven, I moved with my parents and younger sister from Shanghai to Tieniu Village in Pujiang, Chengdu.

At first, I could not settle in. There were few people my age in the village, so after school I often stayed in my room, going online and playing video games to pass the lonely hours. I did not know what this village had to do with me, or why my parents had taken us from a metropolis like Shanghai to somewhere as small as Tieniu Village.

Over time, I realized that the word “village” could never fully describe this place.

There are citrus orchards, fishponds, and farms here, but also a group of young people who have chosen to live with care—the “R'gan Youth.” They, too, left major cities such as Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen. In Tieniu Village, they practice ecological agriculture, grow and cook their own food, and look after the people around them.

Here, I gradually came to understand that air, water, food, and soil are more than words in a textbook.

I breathed freely in the old-growth forest of the tea mountains, grew vegetables in our one-square-metre garden, watered and weeded at the community farm, and watched citrus fruits no bigger than a fingernail slowly grow in the orchard.

I began to see that the food we put into our bodies grows from the soil—and only healthy soil can produce food that truly nourishes us.

Out of these experiences, the idea of R'gan Junior began quietly taking root in me.

R'gan Junior 1.0

At first, I simply wanted friends to visit the village

The earliest version of R'gan Junior was mostly about making friends and widening my circle.

I was very lonely then, and I wanted to connect with people my own age.

During the winter break of eighth grade, I began reaching out to old friends and inviting them into the R'gan Junior community. We talked online, learned about Tieniu Village, and discussed sustainable living—and whether young people could make even small changes within their own communities.

There was no curriculum and no fully formed organization. We were simply curious and enthusiastic, hoping to introduce more of our peers to rural life, nature, and sustainable ways of living.

But we lived in different cities and could not meet in person. After a few online gatherings, the energy gradually faded. R'gan Junior 1.0 did not last very long.

Still, it left a seed in me:

One day, I hoped, more young people would truly come to the village—to spend time together, observe the land together, and ask what we might do for the environment.

R'gan Junior 2.0

Turning an idea into action

In the summer of 2023, I finished middle school in the conventional education system and moved to Chengdu for high school.

At my new school, I met many talented and hardworking classmates. Everyone had goals and was ready to keep moving forward. Yet I felt that, although we absorbed enormous amounts of knowledge and information every day, we rarely had a chance to step into nature or engage with the real world.

I thought of Tieniu Village again.

Rural communities need more young people who care about ecology and the environment. At the same time, growing teenagers like us need nature—a place to relax our bodies and minds and do something real.

Why not bring those two needs together?

That semester, our school invited us to join the China Thinks Big youth innovation challenge. I asked four classmates to relaunch the R'gan Junior project with me.

For the first time, R'gan Junior became more than “bringing friends to the village.” It began to confront a real question: Can young people genuinely participate in rural and ecological issues?

At the beginning, none of us understood rural communities very well. We did not know how to run a project or conduct research, let alone explain the ecological issues that deserved attention. So we started by coming to the village. With guidance from my parents and older friends in the community, we watched, learned, and worked at the same time.

Once our research question became clearer, we invited more than twenty students and parents to Tieniu Village for the Gantian Citrus Harvest Festival. They entered the orchards, learned about ecologically grown citrus, joined our activities, and completed surveys and feedback forms.

We were fortunate to receive support in many forms. Ye Ke introduced everyone to citrus growing. Wenjing took R'gan portraits for the participants. Other community members taught us how to conduct field research, while our teammates' parents helped us run project meetings, organize data, and prepare presentations.

In the end, our CTB project advanced from a field of more than two thousand teams to the Global Forum at Harvard University. Our paper, R'gan Junior: Research and Practice in Participating in Sustainable Agriculture Through a Single Citrus Fruit, was selected for publication in the forum journal. Soon afterward, we were invited to the youth online forum of the Claremont International Forum on Ecological Civilization, where we shared the R'gan Junior story with more people who care about ecology and sustainable development.

That experience left a lasting impression on me:

When even a very small idea takes root in real soil, people will come forward to support it—and it can grow into action.

R'gan Junior 3.0

After the excitement, returning to a deeper understanding

After CTB, I faced a new problem.

As classmates transferred schools, prepared for further study, or followed new plans, many gradually left the project. Their attention to rural communities, ecology, and sustainable agriculture did not continue.

At first, I felt disappointed.

Then I understood that a single competition or event is rarely enough to create lasting change.

I also began to question myself. Was my understanding of rural life, ecological agriculture, and sustainable living still only on the surface? If you truly want to do something, slogans and a handful of project presentations are not enough. You need a much longer period of learning, observing, and practising.

So I began to slow down, enter more deeply into everyday life, and search for an inner ecology.

I began drinking tea and sitting quietly, noticing my breath, body, and emotions.

I began buying groceries and cooking, discovering the calm and joy that came from taking everyday life seriously.

I grew used to walking in the mountains and lying on the grass, listening both to nature and to the voice within me.

I found that as my body relaxed and practical life skills grew, I became happier and more grounded. Once my mind grew quiet, I also had more strength to explore the world beyond myself.

I became an intern at the community's Future Rural Research Institute. I joined an international regenerative-design co-creation camp, helped with a youth rural-development practice camp, and welcomed visiting Yale University scholars and professors as well as middle-school students from Pujiang. From my own perspective, I told every visitor the story of what is unfolding in Tieniu Village.

Beyond joining community projects, my father and I began caring for the four citrus trees beside our home.

I applied what I had learned about observing soil, weeds, insects, and the condition of each tree. I learned when to prune and how to apply organic fertilizer while relying as little as possible on chemical inputs.

Before, when I spoke of “ecological agriculture,” it often sounded like an abstract idea. Only when I stood before those trees—watching them put out leaves, blossom, and bear fruit, while also seeing the challenges of pests, disease, and daily management—did I understand that caring for the land is not a slogan. It is specific, patient work.

At the same time, I began interviewing staff from Pujiang County's agriculture and rural affairs authorities and local practitioners of ecological agriculture, hoping to understand the problems they actually face:

  • Why is ecological agriculture important, yet so difficult to sustain?

  • What is the greatest pressure on farmers who transition to ecological growing?

This year, with guidance from a teacher at school, I also founded a Behavioral Economics Study Group. Together, my classmates and I are studying how urban families think about buying ecological products. Zhiyan, who leads the community's Ganba Ganma membership program, helped us distribute surveys and conduct interviews with member families and other city residents. We wanted to know:

  • Can a young person's ecological actions influence the choices of an entire family?

  • Can a child's understanding of land and food gradually change how their family supports ecological agriculture?

From there, I began to feel that R'gan Junior should be more than simply “bringing people to see the countryside.”

It is closer to an invitation: an invitation to step into the fields and into real life; to first feel our bodies and understand the soil beneath our feet and the food we eat each day; then to ask questions, observe together, and slowly work out whether we, as young people, can do something real for nature, rural communities, and a more sustainable way of life.
