Hello, I'm Tianshi Zhang. I am eighteen years old, live in Zhengzhou, Henan Province, and have just graduated from high school.

I fell in love with programming in middle school. I started an online game-development studio and took part in algorithm-design competitions.

In high school, I went deeper into mathematics and computer science, founded Qiyouxing Technology, and built an online community for developers.

At first glance, none of this seems to have much to do with R-Gan Junior.

Why would a city teenager more at home with the internet, programming, AI, and online communities come to Tieniu Village and join a youth project about land, food, ecological agriculture, and real life?

The answer begins with my family.

My parents joined Tieniu Village’s R-Gan Parents membership program and became seasonal residents. For years, they have worked alongside Nate’s family to help build the rural community.

Through that relationship, a sense of connection and shared purpose slowly grew between me, Tieniu Village, and R-Gan Junior.

From the Online World to Tieniu Village

From elementary school through middle school, I experienced a loneliness much like Nate’s in the village.

I had no friends who shared my interests and with whom I could have deeper conversations. I did not really know how to make friends. My mother often spent time in the countryside and had little time with me.

Like many children in China, I experienced online schooling during my three years of middle school.

During that period, I gradually moved from simply playing games toward a stronger sense of self-discipline. I began to feel that I could not keep spending my time on things without meaning.

So I taught myself programming, built communities, and began experimenting with starting a company.

Looking back, the R-Gan spirit was quietly giving me the strength to keep growing.

I first visited Tieniu Village during the summer after fifth grade, when I joined the community's Magic Doudou Family Camp.

There, I experienced a way of life completely different from the city.

What I remember most was the playground, now called Tianyeli. It had two enormous white bubbles where everyone could relax and play like a child.

The community was just beginning to take shape, and few newcomers lived there year-round. Everyone I met was warm and friendly, leaving a deep impression on me as a young child.

Over time, I saw warm, caring people with ecological ideals and a concern for others building the place where they wanted to live and work. They were also creating a new kind of rural community suited to contemporary life.

Nate and Me: Two Different Paths of Growth

Nate and I played together at Doudou Camp when we were children.

We came to know each other more deeply through our families' summer travels and learning journeys.

Together, we visited Nice and Provence in France, Westlake University in Hangzhou, the studio of the director behind the animated film Jiang Ziya, and many other interesting and meaningful places. We also met many older people who were pursuing their ideals.

Through these journeys, I gradually came to understand that both individuals and communities need a wide horizon. We also need to enter the real world together—to remain open to experience, embrace one another, and keep creating.

I have always lived in cities, immersed in the internet industry and information technology and building online communities for innovative developers. Nate followed his parents into the work of building an ecological community, advocating sustainable ways of living, and exploring the world through a village.

When Nate brought R-Gan Junior to the CTB Global Forum, I began to understand anew something his father once said:

Ecological work involves not only the soil and air around us, but also the sustainability of each person’s inner ecology—the balance between our bodies, emotions, relationships, and everyday lives.

That idea moved me deeply.

Before, I understood ecology and sustainability mostly through ideas. Gradually, I realized that true ecology is also connected to a person's body, food, emotions, relationships, and way of life.

From Understanding with the Mind to Experiencing with the Body

A month ago, after finishing China’s national college entrance examination, I returned to Tieniu Village. I tried Mykunda’s new village-stay experience and saw how much the village had changed.

Centered on the Mykunda ecological community, an entire network of commercial ventures, nonprofits, and social enterprises has grown here.

It reflected ideas of institutional development, platforms, and community-building that I had encountered online. It also embodied a central principle of China's rural-revitalization strategy: people—and the work they are willing to do—are what matter most.

Here, ideals and reality move forward side by side. Seeing them become so tangible and complete affected me deeply.

On the community’s display of member profiles, I saw young people from around the world who shared ecological ideals, including graduates of leading universities. They had chosen to build their lives in the countryside.

Their way of changing the world begins with the soil beneath their feet.

Over five full years, they expanded their ecological restoration from nine mu of orchard (0.6 hectares) to forty-four mu (about 2.93 hectares), and then to 140 mu (about 9.33 hectares). Their work brought Nate and me back to a question we had shared while visiting Westlake University:

When long-term sustainable value and short-term practical value conflict, how should we choose?

The Mykunda community showed me that the things that matter most may not produce results quickly.

But when people keep working, keep adjusting, and choose to stay, both land and community can genuinely change over time.

During those days, I also prepared breakfast, drank tea, harvested crops, and shared daily life with everyone around me.

These experiences were unfamiliar, but they mattered.

I had always been more comfortable solving problems with a computer, code, and online tools. In Tieniu Village, I began to understand something else:

We also need to learn everyday life skills, eat well, and work with others. We need to slow down over a cup of tea or a meal, or while spending time on the land.

R-Gan Junior as I See It

To me, R-Gan Junior is not simply a group of students pursuing ecology and public service, nor is it a roster of event participants.

It is a community shaped by people of different backgrounds, ages, and genders who grow together in good health and share everyday life.

Some care about ecological agriculture.

Some care about public policy.

Some love music and art.

Some are drawn to technology and AI.

And some simply want to leave their rooms and rediscover nature and everyday life.

None of us has to know, at the beginning, exactly what we can contribute.

But once we step into the real world and begin to live, observe, question, and act, we gradually find our place.

I feel fortunate that, as I grew, I was able to witness and experience a real village in western Sichuan slowly becoming the ecological home so many people dreamed of—because a group of people kept working toward it.

I am more certain than ever that working with like-minded partners on something meaningful to society is the most valuable form of innovation in our time.

How I Understand R-Gan Junior 1.0, 2.0, and 3.0

As the founder of R-Gan Junior, Nate describes the project in three stages:

In 1.0, community families nurtured and supported the project.

In 2.0, papers intended to support further education and competition experience were central considerations.

In 3.0, participants began initiating research projects and organizing activities on their own.

This is how I understand those stages:

1.0 was nurtured by fertile ground and outside support.

2.0 brought together like-minded people.

3.0 reconnected with our deepest motivations through sustained work, personal growth, and shared understanding.

As my understanding deepened, I gradually found my way into R-Gan Junior.

I once grasped the value of ecological sustainability mainly through ideas. Now, I hope to feel for myself the power of natural growth and the unity of knowledge and action—in every plant in the forest, every vegetable and meal in the community, and every warm sip of tea.

What I Can Do as a Young Technologist

This time, I am happy to join as a Co-creator of the R-Gan Junior Life Co-creation Camp.

Among the four co-creators who launched the camp together, I may bring a different perspective.

I am more familiar with technology, AI, programming, and online communities, and I have some experience organizing teams and projects.

Yet that is exactly why I can feel how many young people today share circumstances like mine:

We know screens, information, and tools. But we may not truly know the land, our food, our bodies, or the work of everyday life.

So there is one thing I hope to do within R-Gan Junior: bring technology back into real life, and bring AI and digital tools into the places where people work with the land.

At this co-creation camp, I will work with other participants in the science and technology group. Together, we will ask:

  • Can technology support rural communities instead of taking us further from real life?

  • Can AI help organize information and interviews about ecological tangerines?

  • Can short videos, online communities, and digital tools help more urban families understand land and ecological agriculture?

  • Can we use the skills we learn online to offer even a little practical help to a rural community?

I do not believe technology and nature must stand in opposition. What matters is how we choose to use technology.

It can pull people deeper into their screens, but it can also help them understand land, food, and human relationships anew.

If Nate grew R-Gan Junior from Tieniu Village and its soil, I hope to bring the project another source of strength—from technology and community-building.
