Hi everyone, I'm Ruorong!

In the summer of 2022, when I was twelve, I moved from Shanghai to Los Angeles and began going to school and building a life in the United States.

It was a major transition: a new language environment, surrounded by people with different cultural backgrounds and ways of living.

Before the move, I had taken a year away from school for health reasons. Coming to America gave me a new opportunity to understand myself and learn how to live.

Faced with an unfamiliar language and environment, I chose to try things bravely.

I joined choir and the debate team, and explored martial arts, horseback riding, basketball, volleyball, dance, and photography. I wanted to give myself the chance to encounter different things and, through each new attempt, gradually discover who I was.

In the end, I chose to devote more time to riding, photography, and debate, because there I found both the things I loved and a circle of friends.

Even now, I am still learning how to balance my health, studies, interests, and everyday life. I hope to stay healthy while giving my full attention—and real enjoyment—to everything I love.

Although living in America has introduced me to many cultures, traditional Chinese culture remains one of the deepest parts of my upbringing.

When I was young, my family took me on learning journeys across China. I studied calligraphy, traditional Chinese painting, and martial arts, and I also loved making and drinking tea.

For me, traditional Chinese culture is a way of life that has accompanied me as I grew. It is also what continues to motivate me to practise Chinese painting and study the Chinese language today.

The Tea Mountain and Me

In 2021, during the year I had stepped away from school because of my health, my maternal grandfather—whom I call Agong—took me to the tea mountain for the first time. Together, we entered this forest filled with life.

It felt as if I had discovered a new world. I was so happy there, and I planted young tea seedlings with Agong.

That journey planted a seed in my heart.

There, I discovered that a person could live in an entirely different way.

You could spend each day in nature, quietly and simply among the mountains and trees; prepare every bowl of tea with care; and pay close attention to each passing moment.

I was still very young, but this way of life moved me deeply.

Traditional Chinese culture had surrounded me from an early age.

My family often took me on learning journeys throughout China. I studied calligraphy and traditional Chinese painting, and I loved making and drinking tea.

Black tea, green tea, white tea, pu'er—I encountered many kinds of tea and always thought I loved it. But at first, that love simply meant enjoying how it tasted.

Only when my grandfather brought me tea made from the old, wild-growing trees on the mountain did I learn that tea could be experienced in another way.

Every bowl of tea became a meeting between body and mind.

Each time I quietly prepared and drank tea, I noticed the smallest changes in my body. I gradually learned to listen to it, feel my breath, and let myself slow down.

Arriving at the tea mountain felt like one of fate's quiet arrangements. I met a group of kind and fascinating older friends, as well as teachers.

Their sincerity, warmth, and calm left a deep impression on me, even though I was only ten.

I drank tea that truly nourished both body and mind. I felt that a person could form a deep and genuine connection with their own body—and with nature.

The warmth I found there made me fall in love with this energetic forest.

Later, I moved to the United States. There, I met another tea friend, Mina.

Mina has always lived in America, yet she returns to the tea mountain almost every year to pick and make tea by hand during the harvest season.

Even while pregnant, she still made the journey because she treasured her sincere and profound connection with tea.

Today, she teaches tea in the United States, sharing traditional Chinese tea culture with people from many countries and cultural backgrounds.

She guides them in preparing and tasting tea and experiencing the stillness a single bowl can bring. Through her, more people come to see tea as a way of being with oneself, with others, and with nature.

Watching her, I realized that the tea mountain connects people all over the world.

No matter where we come from, if we truly draw close to a bowl of tea, we can feel the warmth, wisdom, and vitality it carries.

In 2025, Agong passed away.

It was the first time I had truly faced life and death. I felt helpless, heartbroken, and lost, unsure how to live with the pain of losing someone so close to me.

Yet connection and circumstance brought me back to the tea mountain once more.

That August, I joined a Zen tea course. Unlike the online courses I had taken before, this time I entered the mountain itself—and found my way back to myself.

The Zen tea course helped me understand more deeply the relationship between tea, body, and mind. It also led me to think again about life and death.

I began to understand that Agong had completed his journey in this world. He had reached the end of this part of life's path, but that did not mean he had simply left us.

What he gave me was more than memory. He left me this bond with tea—and a kind of wisdom for facing life itself.

Without Agong, I would never have known the tea mountain or met so many kind people. I would not have learned to understand my body, or slowly found a way to live with missing him after he was gone.

Now, when I lift a bowl of tea, I notice more than its fragrance. I can feel Agong still beside me, and I can feel the warmth, calm, and strength the forest has given me.

I also hope that one day, like Mina, I can share the beauty of the tea mountain with more people—so that through a bowl of tea, they might rediscover their bodies, encounter nature, and feel the most sincere connection between one person and another.
