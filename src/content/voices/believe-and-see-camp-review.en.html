Believe, and You Will See

“Wild Mint” reflects on the R-Gan Junior Life Co-creation Camp

On July 4, Tianshi and I sent out this invitation:

“This summer, we want to invite a group of people our age into forests, fields, and the everyday life of a real community. To run, breathe, cook, and get to know the land beneath our feet and the food we eat each day. And to talk with the people beside us: can we do something real for nature, our communities, and the way we will live in the future?”

On July 28, ten families and eleven young people from seven cities in China and the United States came to Nanbao Mountain and Tieniu Village.

We ranged in age from eleven to eighteen, and most of us had never met. Over five days, we walked through forests, picked tea, played ball, and cooked together. We also worked out an agreement for living together and shared life in the same community.

Everyone at camp chose a nature name. Mine was “Wild Mint.” “Little Tree,” “Honey,” “Water,” “Water Pine,” and “Moss” were among the people I lived with during those five days. “Passion Fruit” was one of the teachers supporting the camp.

At first, I felt that, as a founder, I should take the initiative, look after everyone, and make sure nobody was left out.

Only later did I realize that, before making any arrangements, the first thing I needed to learn was to trust them.

01

“Little Tree” did not need me to speak for him

On the first evening, we discussed our agreement for living together.

I was in a group with “Little Tree” and “Moss.”

“Little Tree” was quiet. I worried that he might not keep up with the discussion, or know what to say when it was his turn to share. I had even decided that, if he got stuck, I would step in and carry on for him.

Before his turn came, I held back. I simply checked where we had reached in the discussion, then waited for him to speak for himself.

As it turned out, “Little Tree” spoke fluently and organized his thoughts clearly.

Some of his ideas were more mature than I had expected.

Sitting beside him, I felt surprised—and a little embarrassed.

Just because he was quiet, I had already assumed that he might struggle to express himself.

I had underestimated him.

“Little Tree” had ideas of his own. He simply needed a little time, and people around him who were willing to wait.

Later, in an interview, I said:

“Giving them space was really about letting go of my own need to step in.”

I was letting go of the part of me that wanted to step in as soon as someone took a little longer.

02

“Honey’s” basket did not need to be filled for him

On the second day, we went tea picking.

By midsummer, the tea leaves had grown a little old. Some of the younger participants had not picked much, so they put all their leaves into Honey’s basket, hoping to make at least one basket look fuller.

It still was not enough.

Later, I saw adults adding more and more tea leaves to his basket.

Of course, everyone meant well. After a whole morning’s work, they wanted the children to have something to show for it.

“Honey” walking into the tea fields

“Honey” receiving tea leaves from the others

A basket full of fresh leaves

But afterward, I kept thinking about that basket.

What if, instead of putting leaves straight into it, we had walked a little farther with him?

We could have searched and looked together, found suitable leaves, and let him pick them himself.

He might still have ended up with very few.

But every leaf in the basket would have been one they had found and picked for themselves.

During our reflection, two phrases kept returning to me:

Do it for them, or do it with them.

Finishing the task for someone is quick.

Doing it alongside them keeps them involved. They use their own hands, make choices, and know that part of the result belongs to them.

Next time, I want to ask myself one more question: am I helping someone take a step forward, or am I taking away a step that should have been theirs?

03

“Water” took her place among us

On the fourth day, we prepared lunch together.

“Water” was one of the younger participants. At first, she did not say much, and she had not done a great deal in the kitchen before.

If it had been the first day, I probably would have given her a simple, safe task that was hard to get wrong.

After spending a few days together, I knew that she was conscientious and willing to take things on.

That day, I handed her the cucumbers.

“Water” getting ready to cut the cucumbers

I explained how to cut them into irregular chunks by turning the cucumber between diagonal cuts, and told her to ask whenever she needed help.

For more than an hour, she barely stopped. She peeled and cut cucumbers, then offered to help with other tasks as well.

Some jobs were fiddly and some were tiring. She was willing to do them all.

She was not standing on the sidelines, waiting for the older participants to look after her. She was right there among us, working alongside everyone else.

When the food was ready, we lined up to introduce each dish.

Water was there too.

I suddenly felt very happy.

She was more than “one of the younger children.” She was a partner who had helped make the meal. Part of the lunch on the table was hers.

04

I began to see each person

Of course, “Little Tree,” “Honey,” and “Water” were not the only people I came to see during those five days.

I think of how “Water Pine” stood by his friends.

For a friend, he was willing to try things he did not know well.

I think of Mimosa’s sincerity.

With her, there was little need to guess what she meant.

I also think of the ideas “Baby’s Breath” would suddenly come up with.

She always brought a little surprise to whatever we were doing.

And there was Moss’s sense of responsibility.

He took things seriously and followed through, often without needing a reminder.

Leopard Cat’s good manners also stayed with me.

He was tall, but his respect for others often showed in very small gestures.

I think of Radish’s maturity too.

He had his own judgment and knew when to step forward.

It was only when we said goodbye that I understood how deeply “Orange” valued our friendship.

Five days had been enough to make a group of strangers reluctant to part.

When camp began, I was quick to notice someone’s age or personality, or whether they seemed to “need help.”

Five days later, I no longer saw categories of people. I saw individuals.

Everyone has a rhythm of their own, and things that may not be visible at first. Given a little time, those things slowly emerge.

05

Clear boundaries make room for freedom

I also began to understand that trust does not mean letting go of every limit.

At camp, we had an agreement for living together, safety boundaries, and responsibilities we needed to share. Once those boundaries were clear, much of the space within them could belong to the participants themselves.

Let “Little Tree” speak for himself.

Let “Honey” pick his own leaves.

Let “Water” take real responsibility for something.

Of course, we can offer reminders, companionship, or a helping hand when needed. But often, a child does not need us to do everything for them.

They need someone willing to believe that they have something of their own to offer.

Many abilities do not appear only after we have taught them.

They are already there. They simply need a little room.

06

I am learning to trust myself too

These moments also made me reconsider what kind of founder I wanted to be.

At first, I thought a founder should stand at the front, arrange everything, and solve every problem.

As the days passed, I became less preoccupied with the idea that “I have to lead everyone.”

I talked, joked, and played basketball with them. I also sat beside them and listened.

Once I relaxed, we actually grew closer.

When something needed organizing, I could step forward.

At other times, I was simply one of the group.

Before, I found it easier to trust other people than to trust myself.

I liked clear arrangements and wanted everything to stay within the plan. When things were uncertain, I often worried that I would not do well enough.

But those five days showed me that trusting others and trusting myself have something in common.

Neither depends on knowing exactly how things will turn out.

Even without knowing, I can still be willing to take a step forward.

At the start of camp, “Passion Fruit” shared three principles with us. The first was:

“We believe that everyone is the best version of themselves they can be at this moment.”

At the time, I did not fully understand it.

Now, when I think of those words, I think of “Little Tree.”

He does not have to become outgoing before he deserves to be heard with care.

I think of “Honey.”

His basket does not have to be filled by someone else for tea picking to have been worthwhile.

I think of “Water.”

Being younger does not mean she must always be given only the simplest tasks.

I think of the others too.

They do not have to prove themselves before they deserve our trust.

Often,

all we need to do is assume a little less and wait a little longer.

Make the boundaries clear, and leave room within them.

Trust that each person will grow, gradually, in their own way.

That is true of my companions.

It is true of me too.

Believe, and you will see.
